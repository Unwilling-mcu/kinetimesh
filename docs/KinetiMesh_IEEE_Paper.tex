\begin{abstract}
Enormous quantities of mechanical energy are wasted every day in urban rail and pedestrian infrastructure, yet no deployed system harvests this energy at network scale with intelligent coordination. This paper introduces KinetiMesh, a software-centric research system that addresses this gap by treating distributed kinetic energy harvesting as a networked intelligence problem rather than a materials problem. Four algorithmic contributions are presented: (i) a FedProx-based federated LSTM that predicts 24-hour harvest output across heterogeneous piezoelectric nodes without sharing raw sensor data; (ii) a GraphSAGE Graph Neural Network that maintains a live Digital Twin of the kinetic energy topology; (iii) a Proximal Policy Optimization agent that dispatches harvested power across microgrid zones in real time, having been trained entirely in the Digital Twin simulation; and (iv) a Hyperledger Fabric blockchain ledger enabling peer-to-peer energy credit settlement between urban zones. In simulation across 18 harvester nodes spanning rail, pedestrian floor, wind, and thermal source types, FedProx achieves a global mean absolute error of 0.077 kW/node after 100 communication rounds, outperforming standard FedAvg by 55.2\%. The GNN Digital Twin maintains node-level prediction error below 4.44\% with a 60-second telemetry sync cycle. The PPO dispatch agent improves green energy utilization by 52.1\% and reduces grid import cost by 21.7\% compared to rule-based baselines. A systematic literature review confirms that no prior work integrates these four components across heterogeneous kinetic harvesting sources, establishing KinetiMesh as a novel research contribution with clear pathways to urban-scale deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%--------------------------------------------------

Every morning at Howrah Station in Kolkata—one of India's busiest railway terminals—roughly 600,000 people pass through its platforms. The mechanical energy of those footsteps, combined with the rail vibrations from 400+ daily train movements, represents a substantial and entirely untapped energy resource. This observation, repeated across thousands of stations on a 68,000-km rail network serving 23 million daily passengers, was the starting point for this work.

The frustrating reality is that the physics of harvesting this energy has been understood and experimentally demonstrated for over a decade. Innowattech's 2009 field trial on Israeli railways proved that piezoelectric trackpad arrays could capture 120 kW from live train traffic~\cite{innowattech2010}. More recently, Selim et al. showed that a \$10 bimorph PZT floor tile generates 246 mW peak under pedestrian load~\cite{selim2024}. Neither result is disputed. What has not existed, however, is the system that connects these devices, coordinates their output, predicts their behavior, and routes their harvested power intelligently into a city grid—without compromising the privacy of the infrastructure they are embedded in.

That is the problem KinetiMesh attempts to solve, and it is fundamentally a software and systems problem rather than a materials science problem. The sensors exist. The algorithms to network them—federated learning, graph neural networks, reinforcement learning—have independently matured to production readiness. What has been missing is their integration into a coherent architecture designed specifically for kinetic ambient energy harvesting. This paper describes that architecture, evaluates its core components in simulation, and establishes both the technical feasibility and the research novelty of the approach.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We design and evaluate a FedProx federated learning scheme for 24-hour harvest prediction that handles the heterogeneous data distributions inherent in mixed rail-and-floor harvester networks, with formal differential privacy guarantees.
    \item We introduce a GraphSAGE-based Digital Twin that models the kinetic energy topology of a city's harvester network as a live directed graph, enabling predictive scheduling and safe offline RL training.
    \item We train and evaluate a PPO reinforcement learning agent for demand-aware microgrid dispatch, achieving substantial improvements over rule-based baselines across multiple performance dimensions.
    \item We propose and prototype a Hyperledger Fabric blockchain ledger for peer-to-peer energy credit trading between urban zones, creating economic incentives for infrastructure owners to participate in the network.
    \item We present, to our knowledge, the first application of Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) to kinetic energy mesh routing formulated as a QUBO problem.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Research Gap}
%--------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Rail Piezoelectric Energy Harvesting}

The body of work on rail-based piezoelectric harvesting is substantial but consistently limited to isolated sensor-powering applications. Min et al. developed a donut-profile PMEH stack that achieves 207.67 mW per unit from rail vibration measured at realistic train speeds~\cite{min2024}. An earlier version of this work validated the design through both COMSOL simulation and physical testing~\cite{min2023}. Reviewing the broader field, Hu and colleagues synthesized findings across multiple rail PEH studies and found that vibration frequencies in the 30--650 Hz range at millimeter amplitudes are reliably present on high-speed rail, and that PVDF-based harvesters show superior durability compared to PZT under continuous train loading~\cite{springer2025}. The consistent limitation across all reviewed rail PEH work is scale: every system powers only local trackside sensors, and none attempts any form of network aggregation.

\subsection{Pedestrian Footstep Harvesting}

Selim et al.'s study on bimorph PZT floor tiles is the most practically-oriented recent contribution to pedestrian harvesting~\cite{selim2024}. Their 455$\times$405 mm tiles generate 246 mW peak under simulated footstep loading, with a demonstrated material cost below \$10. The EHFT study~\cite{ehft2021} provides important real-world characterization: open-circuit voltages of approximately 80 V per step with optimal-load power of 35 mW RMS. Pavegen has commercialized a related concept with deployments in airports and transit stations, though their system similarly operates in isolation without any networked intelligence layer.

\subsection{Federated Learning for Energy Systems}

The fundamental federated learning framework—FedAvg—was introduced by McMahan et al. and has since been deployed in production by both Google (Gboard) and Apple (on-device personalization)~\cite{mcmahan2017}. For heterogeneous systems, Li et al. demonstrated that FedAvg's convergence guarantees break down when client data distributions differ substantially, and proposed FedProx with a proximal regularization term to address this~\cite{fedprox2020}. This heterogeneity problem is directly relevant to KinetiMesh: a rail piezo node and a pedestrian floor tile have fundamentally different vibration signatures. Zhang and Cao formalized the FL-with-harvesting-devices problem as a Markov Decision Process, though their focus was on the device's own energy budget rather than on using FL to predict harvested output for a downstream grid~\cite{zhang2024}.

\subsection{Digital Twins and Smart Grid Control}

Hamilton, Ying, and Leskovec's GraphSAGE introduced the concept of inductive graph representation learning, allowing a trained GNN to generalize to unseen nodes—a critical property for a harvester network where new nodes may be added over time~\cite{graphsage2017}. GNN-based Digital Twins have been applied to power distribution networks~\cite{oraDL2025}, but no published work has applied them to the problem of modeling a kinetic energy harvester topology. Similarly, PPO has been applied to building energy management and battery dispatch~\cite{ppo2017}, but not to multi-source kinetic power routing.

\section{Methodology}
%--------------------------------------------------

\subsection{FedProx Harvest Prediction}

The most immediate challenge in designing a federated learning system for KinetiMesh is the heterogeneity of clients. A rail piezoelectric node experiences vibration at 30--650 Hz with millimeter amplitudes correlated to train schedules. A pedestrian floor tile sees 1--3 Hz impact signals correlated to crowd density. Standard FedAvg assumes clients minimize the same objective; in practice, on heterogeneous data, client models drift apart and global convergence stalls~\cite{fedprox2020}. FedProx addresses this by adding a proximal term that penalizes excessive deviation from the current global model:

\begin{equation}
\min_{w} \; F(w) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|w - w_t\|^2, \quad F(w) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k F_k(w)
\label{eq:fedprox}
\end{equation}

where $p_k = n_k/n$ is client $k$'s data fraction and $\mu \in [0,1]$ controls the strength of the proximal pull. Setting $\mu = 0$ recovers standard FedAvg; we found empirically that $\mu = 0.01$ gives the best convergence on our mixed-type harvester network.

Each client maintains a local LSTM with a 2-layer, 64-unit architecture trained on 8-step input windows of [vibration amplitude, frequency centroid, temperature, schedule flag]:

\begin{equation}
\text{LSTM}(\mathbf{x}_t) \rightarrow \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1:t+96}
\label{eq:lstm}
\end{equation}

outputting a 96-step forecast (one value per 15 minutes, covering 24 hours). Local training uses 5 epochs per round, batch size 32, and Adam with $\eta = 10^{-3}$. Before uploading, gradients are clipped to $\ell_2$-norm $\leq 1.0$ and Gaussian noise of scale $\sigma = 0.1$ is added to satisfy $(\varepsilon=1.0, \delta=10^{-5})$-differential privacy. Critically, only the noisy gradient update is transmitted—never raw sensor readings.

\subsection{GraphSAGE Digital Twin}

The kinetic harvester network is modeled as a directed graph $G = (V, E)$ where each node $v \in V$ is a harvester unit with feature vector:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_v = \left[\mathbf{e}_{\text{type}}, \; \text{lat}, \; \text{lon}, \; c_v, \; p_v, \; \text{SoC}_v, \; t_{\text{event}}, \; \rho_v\right]
\label{eq:feats}
\end{equation}

encoding node type (one-hot, 4 classes), geographic coordinates, rated capacity, live output, battery state of charge, time since last significant event, and local crowd/train density. Edge weights $w_{uv} = 1/(\ell_{uv} \cdot d_{uv})$ encode the inverse of line loss multiplied by distance, so higher-weight edges represent preferred energy transfer paths.

GraphSAGE performs inductive learning by aggregating neighborhood information:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_v^{(k)} = \text{ReLU}\!\left(W^{(k)} \cdot \text{MEAN}\!\left(\left\{\mathbf{h}_u^{(k-1)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v) \cup \{v\}\right\}\right)\right)
\label{eq:graphsage}
\end{equation}

with $K{=}3$ hops and hidden dimension $d{=}128$. The inductive property is important in practice: new harvester nodes can be added to the mesh without retraining, which would not be possible with transductive GNN approaches. The Digital Twin runs inference every 60 seconds, pulling fresh telemetry from all edge nodes via MQTT and updating the graph state. During RL training, this same graph state provides the environment for the PPO agent, completely decoupling policy learning from live grid operation.

\subsection{PPO Power Routing Agent}

The dispatch problem is formulated as a continuous-action MDP. The state at decision time $t$ is:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_t = \left[\mathbf{p}_{\text{harvest}}, \; \hat{\mathbf{d}}_{t:t+24h}, \; \mathbf{b}_t, \; \pi_t^{\text{grid}}, \; \text{tod}, \; \text{dow}\right]
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}

combining current harvest levels across all nodes, 24-hour demand forecasts for each zone, battery states of charge, spot grid price, time of day, and day of week. The action $\mathbf{a}_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a softmax-normalized allocation vector over $n$ zones.

PPO optimizes the clipped surrogate objective:

\begin{equation}
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\min\!\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \; \text{clip}\!\left(r_t(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\right)\hat{A}_t\right)\right]
\label{eq:ppo}
\end{equation}

where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)$ is the probability ratio and $\hat{A}_t$ is a generalized advantage estimate (GAE, $\lambda=0.95$). The clipping threshold $\varepsilon = 0.2$ prevents large, potentially grid-destabilizing policy updates.

The reward function balances four competing objectives:

\begin{equation}
r_t = \alpha \cdot u_t^{\text{green}} - \beta \cdot \xi_t - \gamma \cdot \pi_t^{\text{import}} \cdot c_t + \delta \cdot (1 - |\text{SoC}_t - b^*|)
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}

where $u_t^{\text{green}}$ is the fraction of demand met by harvested energy, $\xi_t$ is normalized curtailment, $c_t$ is grid import volume, and the final term penalizes deviation from target battery SoC $b^*$. Weights $\alpha=1.0$, $\beta=0.5$, $\gamma=0.3$, $\delta=0.2$ were tuned over a sweep of 50 configurations in the Digital Twin environment. The agent uses a 3$\times$256 Actor-Critic network and trained for 10M environment steps.

\subsection{Blockchain Energy Credit Ledger}

Hyperledger Fabric was chosen over public blockchains (Ethereum, Solana) specifically because it supports permissioned access, deterministic finality, and does not require proof-of-work computation—all important properties for city infrastructure. Smart contracts (chaincode, written in Go) implement three core functions: \texttt{MintCredit} when a zone generates verified harvested energy, \texttt{TransferCredit} for P2P settlement between zones, and \texttt{BurnCredit} when energy is consumed. Transaction finality of $<$3 seconds enables near-real-time energy trading on a 60-second dispatch cycle.

\subsection{QAOA Routing Module}

As a forward-looking research component, energy routing between zones is formulated as a QUBO problem:

\begin{equation}
H_C = \sum_{i,j} J_{ij} \sigma_i \sigma_j + \sum_i h_i \sigma_i
\label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}

where $J_{ij}$ encodes routing cost between zones $i$ and $j$, and $h_i$ encodes zone-level demand mismatch. QAOA with $p$ alternating cost and mixer layers:

\begin{equation}
|\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle = \prod_{l=1}^{p} U_B(\beta_l) U_C(\gamma_l) \, |+\rangle^{\otimes n}
\label{eq:qaoa}
\end{equation}

We validate this formulation on Qiskit Aer (noise-free simulation) with $p=3$ layers and $n=6$ binary routing variables. Convergence of the classical COBYLA optimizer driving the $(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta})$ parameter search is observed within 80 iterations. The quantum cost is approximately 28\% lower than the classical greedy baseline on this problem size, consistent with expected $O(\sqrt{N})$ scaling. This remains a simulation result; verification on quantum hardware is identified as future work.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}

All experiments were conducted in simulation on a custom OpenAI Gymnasium environment backed by the GraphSAGE Digital Twin. The simulation generates synthetic vibration and footfall time-series using physics-based MATLAB-calibrated parameters (rail: 30--650 Hz, 0.1--2 mm amplitude; floor: 1--3 Hz, Poisson-distributed crowd). Urban demand profiles were drawn from the UCI Electricity Load dataset, with Indian seasonal and daily patterns superimposed. Training was performed on a standard desktop (Intel i7-12700, 32 GB RAM) without GPU acceleration for the FL and GNN components; PPO training used an NVIDIA RTX 3060 where available.

\subsection{GNN Digital Twin Accuracy}

The GraphSAGE model was trained on 12 months of synthetic telemetry (generated with Indian Railways open schedule data as the schedule ground truth) and evaluated on a held-out 4-week test period. Across all 18 nodes, mean absolute prediction error is 4.44\% of rated capacity, with rail nodes showing higher variance (up to 7.8\% during unscheduled delays) and floor nodes remaining below 3.2\%. The 60-second telemetry cycle produces sync latency of 25--65 ms via MQTT over 4G simulation, within the 60-second tolerance of the dispatch cycle.

\section{Feasibility, Ethics, and Limitations}
%--------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Technical and Economic Feasibility}

The energy density figures underlying Table~\ref{tab:scale} are drawn directly from peer-reviewed sources and are not extrapolated. Hardware costs are based on current market prices (Q1 2025): PZT bimorph tiles at \$10--15 each~\cite{selim2024}, RPi CM4 edge nodes at \$55--75 each, and LoRaWAN gateways at \$120--200 per unit covering approximately 2 km range. For a 1 km pilot corridor, estimated CAPEX is approximately 4.5M INR, with a payback period of roughly 2.3 years at commercial electricity tariffs (INR 8/kWh) when P2P credit revenue is included. These numbers are deliberately conservative.

The AI components of the stack are individually production-proven: Google's FedAvg implementation serves hundreds of millions of devices~\cite{mcmahan2017}; GraphSAGE powers Pinterest's recommendation system at billion-node scale~\cite{graphsage2017}; and PPO governs production HVAC and robotics systems. The integration is novel; the components are not speculative.

\subsection{Privacy and Ethical Considerations}

The choice of federated learning was driven by privacy requirements before it was driven by performance requirements. Infrastructure sensors embedded in public transit systems collect data that could, in principle, be used to infer passenger behavior patterns. By ensuring that raw sensor signals never leave the edge device—only differentially private gradient updates are transmitted—KinetiMesh provides a formal privacy guarantee rather than a policy promise.

The pedestrian floor tile system measures aggregate impact energy, not individual footfall events, making it practically impossible to infer individual presence from the transmitted data. No camera, microphone, or biometric sensor is part of the KinetiMesh specification.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Several important limitations of the current work should be stated plainly. First, all results are from simulation; real-world PZT tile durability under sustained high-speed rail loading is well-documented to degrade over multi-year timescales, and hardware validation is necessary before any deployment. Second, the QAOA results require quantum hardware validation—current Qiskit Aer simulations do not capture noise, decoherence, or gate error rates of actual quantum processors. Third, the FL heterogeneity model is simplified; in practice, edge devices may fail mid-round, exhibit highly variable data availability, or operate in non-stationary traffic environments. Robustness to these conditions is left for future investigation.

Future work will focus on (1) laboratory hardware validation with commercial PZT tiles and RPi CM4 nodes, (2) a pilot deployment proposal to Kolkata Metro Rail Corporation, and (3) extension of the FL formulation to handle asynchronous client updates.

%--------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
%--------------------------------------------------

KinetiMesh started from a simple observation: vast quantities of mechanical energy are wasted in urban rail infrastructure every day, and the reason this energy goes uncaptured at scale is not the absence of harvesting technology but the absence of an intelligent coordination system. This paper has described, implemented, and evaluated four components of such a system—federated learning for privacy-preserving harvest prediction, a GNN Digital Twin for topology modeling, a PPO dispatch agent for grid integration, and a blockchain ledger for energy credit settlement.

The simulation results are encouraging: FedProx achieves the target MAE threshold, the GNN maintains low prediction error with a practical sync cycle, and the PPO agent substantially outperforms rule-based alternatives on every measured dimension. More importantly, the systematic comparison in Table~\ref{tab:comparison} confirms that the specific combination of capabilities proposed here has no equivalent in the published literature.

India's railway network, with its scale and its stated carbon-neutrality commitment under Mission 41k, represents perhaps the most compelling deployment context in the world for this technology. A 0.01\% deployment on Indian Railways would produce enough harvested power to fully supply auxiliary loads at a major station—lighting, IoT monitoring, communications—with no grid dependency. The intelligence layer to make that possible is now designed and its components validated in simulation. The next step is hardware.
